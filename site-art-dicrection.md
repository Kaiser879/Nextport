# Portfolio site visual direction

## Creative direction

Build a personal portfolio that feels like a quiet, cinematic project archive: atmospheric and editorial, with the clarity of a well-organized case-study index. Carry over the references’ restrained palette, dramatic negative space, thin technical marks, and contrast between expressive imagery and precise typography. The portfolio should still make the owner’s role, work, and contact path obvious at a glance.

The mood is thoughtful, confident, and slightly mysterious. Keep the visual drama in the project art and page composition; keep navigation and reading straightforward.

## Color system

Use the colors as a flexible system, not as exact sampled brand values:

| Role | Color | Application |
|---|---|---|
| Warm paper | `#F3F1E9` | Main page background and generous open space |
| Near-black | `#111313` | Main text, dark panels, navigation, and strong rules |
| Muted teal | `#456260` | Hero atmosphere, selected project imagery, and restrained emphasis |
| Slate green | `#71827B` | Secondary text, subtle borders, and atmospheric surfaces |
| Soft white | `#F7F6F1` | Cards or title blocks over dark imagery |
| Graphite | `#55514A` | Body copy, captions, and metadata |
| Weathered ochre | `#A9783F` | Small highlights for tags, dates, or active states |

Keep saturation low. Use the warm paper background as the default canvas, near-black for contrast, and teal or ochre sparingly. Project art may introduce its own colors, but surrounding UI should remain quiet.

## Typography

- Choose a clean sans-serif with a neutral, contemporary shape. Use a restrained weight range rather than mixing many type styles.
- Set the owner’s name or portfolio headline large and light, with measured letter spacing. It can occupy multiple lines and sit partly within the hero artwork, as long as it remains readable.
- Use compact uppercase labels with generous tracking for section markers, project category, year, and navigation details.
- Use a comfortable regular-weight size and line length for introductions and case-study descriptions; avoid making all supporting text tiny and widely tracked.
- Build hierarchy with size, weight, line breaks, and spacing. Avoid decorative display fonts and overly tight tracking.

## Page structure and composition

### 1. Header

A slim header sits on the paper background with a small name or monogram at left, a compact role or location line nearby, and a few links at right (Work, About, Contact). Use a thin divider or a tiny registration mark as a quiet graphic detail. Keep the header easy to scan and usable on small screens.

### 2. Hero

Create a broad, cinematic opening panel with a portfolio headline, one short sentence describing the owner’s practice, and a clear “View selected work” link. Use one strong image or a collage from actual portfolio projects. Compose it asymmetrically: keep a calmer, lighter text zone on one side and let the image or darker teal atmosphere gather on the other. A soft haze, subtle grain, or brush-like edge can suggest the source artwork without obscuring the content.

Add a small index label such as “Independent designer / 2020—26” and a quiet scroll cue. Do not place important text over visually busy parts of the image.

### 3. Selected work

Present projects as an editorial sequence rather than a dense grid of uniform cards. Each entry should include a large visual, project name, concise description, role, and year. Alternate image placement or crop to create rhythm while keeping the text alignment and metadata consistent. Use thin rules, numbered labels, and restrained tags to organize the list.

Images should be the focal points. Give them consistent outer spacing and purposeful crops; allow occasional full-bleed or tall images to create emphasis. Hover or focus states may reveal a small arrow, title shift, or subtle image movement, but the project link must be clear without interaction.

### 4. About and capabilities

Use a quieter paper-colored section with a short first-person introduction and a compact list of capabilities, disciplines, or tools. A portrait or work-in-progress image may appear as a secondary element. Keep the layout open and typographic; use a partial circle, fine rule, or small coordinate-like label for continuity with the poster references.

### 5. Contact and footer

Close with a bold but simple invitation to get in touch, a visible email link, and any relevant social or professional links. Use a near-black or muted teal field for a final tonal shift, with warm-white typography and one small ochre accent. Keep legal, copyright, and location details understated in the footer.

## Imagery, lighting, and texture

- Favor project-specific photography, renders, illustrations, or screenshots. Treat each image as a small cinematic scene with one clear subject.
- Use soft directional light, deep but detailed shadows, and atmospheric backgrounds where appropriate. Preserve the natural character of each project rather than applying one heavy filter to every asset.
- When images need a unifying treatment, use restrained cool teal shadows, warm highlights, gentle grain, and slightly softened contrast.
- Abstract textures, architecture, brush marks, and blurred light can support the hero or section transitions. Keep decorative effects out of text-heavy areas.
- Maintain crisp typography and UI edges against painterly or photographic imagery.

## Graphic language

- Use thin graphite rules, small circles or partial arcs, tiny index numbers, and compact uppercase metadata.
- Use black or white rectangular label blocks only where they improve hierarchy, such as a title over a hero image.
- Keep corners mostly square or only subtly softened. Avoid generic glossy gradients, loud neon, heavy shadows, and excessive card framing.
- Use a consistent spacing grid, broad margins, and generous vertical gaps to make the site feel deliberate and calm.

## Interaction and accessibility

- Keep navigation, project links, and the contact action obvious and keyboard accessible.
- Use modest transitions: a slow image reveal, subtle opacity shift, or small directional movement. Respect reduced-motion settings.
- Maintain readable text contrast on both paper and dark sections; never rely on color alone to communicate project category or link state.
- On narrow screens, stack content in a clear reading order, preserve comfortable type sizes, and keep project imagery prominent without horizontal overflow.

## Design summary

An editorial portfolio on a warm paper canvas, with confident sans-serif typography, precise metadata, and cinematic project imagery. Pale negative space and cool teal shadows carry the atmosphere; near-black anchors hierarchy, and ochre appears only as a small accent. The result should feel authored and memorable while making the work, the person behind it, and the next action immediately clear.
